In this lesson you will turn the security mindset into a method. You will ask four questions about a system, draw the simplest useful picture of it, and walk a checklist of what can go wrong. Then you will do it on your own Termux install, with an SSH service running and a folder of notes. A threat model is not a document you file away, but a short list of decisions you can follow.

## What you'll be able to do

- Ask the four threat modelling questions about any system, including your phone.
- Draw a data flow diagram in plain text.
- Use STRIDE as a checklist of what can go wrong.
- Turn a model into a short list with a decision beside each item.

## Step 1: Four questions anyone can ask

Threat modelling sounds like specialist work. It is four questions, and you can ask them about anything.

What am I working on? The system, drawn out so you can see its parts. You cannot reason about what you cannot describe.

What can go wrong? Where you look for weaknesses, on purpose and in a fixed order, so you find more than the obvious ones.

What am I going to do about it? The part people skip. For each item you decide: fix it, accept it, or watch it. A model with no decisions is a worry list.

Did I do a good job? The review, later, to check the decisions still hold as the system changes.

## Step 2: Draw what you have

The simplest useful picture is a data flow diagram, and you need no software.

Four shapes cover almost everything. A box is a process, a store is where data sits, an arrow is data moving, and a dashed border is a trust boundary, across which trust is not automatic.

Here is your own setup, drawn in text.

```text
        +----------------+
  you ->|  Termux shell  |
        +--------+-------+
                 |
   ------------- | -------------  (device boundary)
                 |
    +-----------------+   +------------------+
    |  notes folder   |   |  sshd on :8022   |
    |  (data store)   |   |  (a process)     |
    +-----------------+   +------------------+
                                   ^
   another machine ----------------+
```

Read it left to right. You sit at the edge, typing. The shell is the process you drive, the notes folder is a store, and the SSH service listens. The arrow from another machine crosses the device boundary, so that flow is the one to look at first.

## Step 3: Ask what can go wrong, with STRIDE

A fixed checklist stops you seeing only the weakness you already worried about. The usual one is STRIDE, six letters for six kinds of problem.

Spoofing: pretending to be someone or something else.

Tampering: changing data you should not change.

Repudiation: denying an event, with no record to settle it.

Information disclosure: data reaching somewhere it should not.

Denial of service: stopping something from working.

Elevation of privilege: gaining more power than you were given.

Ask each letter of your picture in order. You will skip some; the value is that you looked.

## Step 4: Work it on your own setup

Run STRIDE over the picture from Step 2. Termux with sshd on port 8022 and a folder of notes is familiar, and it produces a real list.

Spoofing. A weak SSH password could be guessed by someone on the same network, and a key with no passphrase on an unlocked stolen phone means they are already you.

Tampering. The notes folder can be written by anything running as you, and in shared storage by other apps too.

Repudiation. Run sshd without logging and a connection leaves no trace you can read.

Information disclosure. A world readable notes folder can be read by another app, and sshd listening on a reachable address is exposed to the network.

Denial of service. A flood of connection attempts uses the battery, and a full disk stops writes to the notes folder.

Elevation of privilege. On a rooted phone a fault could reach more than your app; without root this is much harder, a reason to stay unrooted.

<Callout type="warning" title="A model is a snapshot">
The list above is true for a particular setup on a particular day. Change the setup and the list changes, which is why the last question is a habit rather than a one-off.
</Callout>

## Step 5: Write it down with a decision

The output is a short written list, with a decision next to each item, so the next time you open it you know what you chose and why. Keep it in a file so it survives a restart.

A line per item, each ending in a decision: fix it, accept it, or watch it. A decision you cannot state in a few words is not a decision yet.

```text
asset: sshd on port 8022
threat: an exposed listener anyone on the network can reach
risk: medium, and only while the listener runs
decision: keep sshd stopped unless I am using it

asset: notes folder
threat: readable by another app if permissions are loose
risk: high, the notes are private
decision: keep it in the private home directory, mode 600

asset: device without root
threat: a fault reaching more than my app
risk: low while the phone stays unrooted
decision: accept it, and do not root the device
```

Three items, three decisions, none of them a paragraph. The first two are prevention; the third is a decision to accept a risk on purpose, which is a real answer as long as you wrote down why. A short list you act on beats a thorough one you never open.

## Quick reference

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| The four questions | What am I working on, what can go wrong, what to do about it, did I do a good job |
| Box | A process, something that acts on data |
| Store | Where data sits |
| Arrow | Data moving between two things |
| Trust boundary | A line across which trust is not automatic |
| STRIDE | Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege |
